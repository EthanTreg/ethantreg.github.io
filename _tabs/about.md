## Background

I am a PhD student in the [LASTRO](https://www.epfl.ch/labs/lastro/) department at [EPFL](https://www.epfl.ch/) working with [Dr David Harvey](https://people.epfl.ch/david.harvey) developing interpretable neural networks (NNs) to help solve the mystery of dark matter (DM).
While DM makes up 85% of the matter content in the universe, we know very little about its nature, some possible theories include new particles, low-mass black holes, and modifications to our theory of gravity.
The current standard model for DM is a cold collisionless massive particle, called cold dark matter (CDM).
Self-interacting dark matter (SIDM) is a natural extension of CDM as all known particles can directly or indirectly interact with themselves; therefore, we can test if DM interacts with itself, and if this effect can be measured, it could suggest a new dark force and set of particles. 
Since DM only interacts gravitationally, we are using gravitational lensing of galaxy clusters, the largest gravitationally bound objects in the Universe, due to them being DM dominated.\\
We have several suites of simulations, all with slightly different modelling assumptions, each suite includes a simulated universe of CDM, along with the alternative SIDM hypothesis.
We developed a convolutional NN that can detect SIDM in simulated galaxy clusters in the presence of observational noise, independent of the family of simulations used, demonstrating that it is learning the physics and not simulation artefacts.
However, due to NNs being "black box" algorithms, our [first paper](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202556629) developed a method to identify if the network used the same information in its predictions as what it was trained on, thus providing a measure of prediction confidence.\\
We are now working on applying our method to observations and utilising more interpretable machine learning techniques to identify where the self-interaction signal is coming from, and therefore, provide justification to the NN's conclusion.
